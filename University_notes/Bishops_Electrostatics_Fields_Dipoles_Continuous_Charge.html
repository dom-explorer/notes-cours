Electrostatics: Fields, Dipoles & Continuous Charge

From a single charge to paired charges and electric dipoles; use vector superposition, symmetry, and the binomial expansion before moving to continuous distributions.

00 / Roadmap

The big picture

CENTRAL IDEA

An electric field is a vector. Coulomb’s law supplies the contribution from each source charge; superposition adds the contributions. Symmetry cancels some vector components, approximations reveal the distant field, and integration extends the same idea to continuous charge.

Discrete charges
\(\vec E=\sum_i\vec E_i\)

Add vectors, not just magnitudes.

Continuous sources
\(\vec E=\int d\vec E\)

Let \(dq\) replace a finite point charge.

Our geometry throughout the two-charge examples has charges on the horizontal \(x\)-axis, a midpoint at the origin, and the observation point \(P=(0,z)\) on the vertical \(y\)-axis. The vertical unit vector is \(\hat{\mathbf j}\), following the lecture’s notation.

01 / Fundamentals

Coulomb’s law and vector superposition

The electric field produced by a point charge \(q\) at displacement \(\mathbf r\) from the source to the field point is

\[\boxed{\vec E(\mathbf r)=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf r}}\]

The constant \(k=1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)\) is Coulomb’s constant. The unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf r}\) always points from the charge toward the observation point. It does not reverse just because \(q\) is negative.

Positive source \(q>0\)

The scalar factor \(q\) is positive, so \(\vec E\) points outward, along \(\hat{\mathbf r}\).

Negative source \(q<0\)

The scalar factor \(q\) is negative, so \(\vec E\) points inward, opposite to \(\hat{\mathbf r}\).

\[\boxed{\vec E(\mathbf r)=\sum_i\vec E_i(\mathbf r)=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_i\frac{q_i}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_i|^3}(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_i)}\]

Important: what does “find the electric field” mean?

Unless the question specifically asks for the magnitude, your answer must include both magnitude and direction. The signed charges belong in the formula—do not replace \(q_i\) by \(|q_i|\) when summing electric-field vectors.

Place \(q_1=q\) at \(x=-d/2\) and \(q_2\) at \(x=+d/2\). Evaluate the field at \(P=(0,z)\), with \(z\ge 0\). Switch between two like charges and an opposite-sign pair. The source-to-point unit vectors stay the same even when the sign of the second charge changes.

For both arrangements, the distance from either charge to \(P\) is the same:

\[\boxed{R=\sqrt{z^2+\left(\frac d2\right)^2}}\qquad \sin\theta=\frac{d/2}{R},\quad \cos\theta=\frac zR\]

Here \(\theta\) is measured from the vertical toward the line joining each charge to the observation point.

03 / Worked derivation

Two equal charges \(q_1=q_2=q\)

We want the total field at the point directly above the midpoint. First write the superposition law before inserting any trigonometry:

\[\vec E=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{R^2}\left(\hat{\mathbf R}_1+\hat{\mathbf R}_2\right).\]
  1. Write the unit vectors. Using \(\theta\) measured from the vertical,
    \[\begin{aligned}\hat{\mathbf R}_1&=\sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf i}+\cos\theta\,\hat{\mathbf j},\\\hat{\mathbf R}_2&=-\sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf i}+\cos\theta\,\hat{\mathbf j}.\end{aligned}\]
  2. Use symmetry. The horizontal components are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, so
    \[\hat{\mathbf R}_1+\hat{\mathbf R}_2=2\cos\theta\,\hat{\mathbf j}.\]
  3. Eliminate the angle. From the right triangle, \(\cos\theta=z/R\). Therefore
    \[\vec E=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2q}{R^2}\frac zR\,\hat{\mathbf j}=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2qz}{R^3}\,\hat{\mathbf j}.\]
  4. Substitute \(R\) only at the end.
    \[\boxed{\vec E_{\mathrm{equal}}(0,z)=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2qz}{\left[z^2+(d/2)^2\right]^{3/2}}\,\hat{\mathbf j}}\]

Limit at the midpoint: \(z\to0\)

If \(d>0\) remains fixed, the denominator is finite while the numerator tends to zero:

\[\boxed{\lim_{z\to0}\vec E_{\mathrm{equal}}=\vec0}\]

Physically, the fields from the two equal charges have equal magnitudes and opposite horizontal directions at the midpoint. They cancel exactly, whether \(q\) is positive or negative.

04 / Asymptotic methods

Far field: the binomial expansion

For observations far above the pair, \(z\gg d/2\), the two source charges look almost like one point charge of magnitude \(2q\). We can show this and calculate the first correction.

Binomial approximation

\[(1+x)^n\approx1+nx\qquad(|x|\ll1)\]

We retain the first correction in the small quantity \(x\), not the first power of \(1/z\).

  1. Start with the exact equal-charge field.
    \[\vec E_{\mathrm{equal}}=k\,2qz\left[z^2+\frac{d^2}{4}\right]^{-3/2}\hat{\mathbf j}.\]
  2. Factor out the dominant \(z^2\). This is algebra, not yet an approximation:
    \[\left[z^2+\frac{d^2}{4}\right]^{-3/2}=\frac1{z^3}\left[1+\frac{d^2}{4z^2}\right]^{-3/2}.\]
  3. Identify \(x\) and \(n\). Since \(z>0\), choose \(x=d^2/(4z^2)\ll1\) and \(n=-3/2\). Then
    \[\left[1+\frac{d^2}{4z^2}\right]^{-3/2}\approx1-\frac32\frac{d^2}{4z^2}=1-\frac{3d^2}{8z^2}.\]
  4. Multiply through and separate orders.
    \[\boxed{\vec E_{\mathrm{equal}}\approx\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\underbrace{\frac{2q}{z^2}}_{\text{leading term}}-\underbrace{\frac{3qd^2}{4z^4}}_{\text{first correction}}\right)\hat{\mathbf j}}\]

The leading term decreases as \(z^{-2}\): the field of the combined charge \(2q\). For \(q>0\), the correction is negative: two spatially separated charges contribute less to the upward field than if both charges were placed at the midpoint. The neglected terms begin at order \(z^{-6}\) (for fixed \(q,d\)).

How accurate is the far-field approximation?

Compare the exact field with the leading term and with the leading term plus first correction. The plot uses \(q=d=k=1\).

05 / Worked derivation

Electric dipole: \(q_1=q,\ q_2=-q\)

Keep the geometry and unit vectors exactly as before. This time the second source charge is negative, so its contribution changes sign. Positive \(q\) is on the left; negative \(q\) is on the right.

\[\vec E=\frac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac q{R^2}\left(\hat{\mathbf R}_1-\hat{\mathbf R}_2\right).\]
  1. Subtract the unit vectors.
    \[\begin{aligned}\hat{\mathbf R}_1-\hat{\mathbf R}_2&=(\sin\theta+\sin\theta)\hat{\mathbf i}\\&\quad +(\cos\theta-\cos\theta)\hat{\mathbf j}\\&=2\sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf i}.\end{aligned}\]

    Vertical components cancel; horizontal components point to the right and add.

  2. Eliminate \(\theta\). Because \(\sin\theta=(d/2)/R\),
    \[\vec E=\frac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac q{R^2}\frac dR\hat{\mathbf i}=\frac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{qd}{R^3}\hat{\mathbf i}.\]
  3. Insert the geometric separation.
    \[\boxed{\vec E_{\mathrm{dipole}}(0,z)=\frac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{qd}{\left[z^2+(d/2)^2\right]^{3/2}}\hat{\mathbf i}}\]

At the midpoint \(z=0\)

\[\boxed{\vec E(0,0)=\frac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{8q}{d^2}\hat{\mathbf i}}\]

Both contributions point from the positive charge toward the negative charge, so they add.

Bring the charges together

\[\lim_{d\to0}\vec E(0,z)=\vec0\quad(z>0\ \text{fixed}).\]

With fixed \(q\), opposite charges cancel when they coincide, away from their common location.

Two different limits

The \(d\to0\) limit above assumes \(z>0\) is fixed. At \(z=0\), the field instead grows as \(8kq/d^2\), so it diverges as \(d\to0\). These limits cannot be interchanged at the singular source point.

Far-field expansion of the dipole

The denominator is exactly the one expanded for equal charges. With \(z\gg d/2\), reuse the same factorization:

\[\begin{aligned}\vec E_{\mathrm{dipole}}&=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{qd}{z^3}\left(1+\frac{d^2}{4z^2}\right)^{-3/2}\hat{\mathbf i}\\&\approx\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{qd}{z^3}\left(1-\frac{3d^2}{8z^2}\right)\hat{\mathbf i}.\end{aligned}\]
\[\boxed{\vec E_{\mathrm{dipole}}\approx\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\underbrace{\frac{qd}{z^3}}_{\text{leading term}}-\underbrace{\frac{3qd^3}{8z^5}}_{\text{first correction}}\right)\hat{\mathbf i}}\]

Why \(1/z^3\), instead of \(1/z^2\)?

The net charge is zero, so the ordinary monopole \(1/z^2\) contribution cancels. The leading field comes from the charge separation and falls off as \(1/z^3\). The scalar \(qd\) is the magnitude of the dipole moment.

Vector convention: electric dipole moment \(\mathbf p=\sum_iq_i\mathbf r_i\) points from negative to positive charge. For our left-positive/right-negative arrangement, \(\mathbf p=-qd\,\hat{\mathbf i}\). On the perpendicular bisector, the field is opposite to \(\mathbf p\), which explains the \(+\hat{\mathbf i}\) direction above.

07 / From sums to integrals

Continuous charge distributions

When the source charge fills a line, surface, or volume, divide it into infinitesimal charge elements \(dq\). Each element contributes a tiny vector field \(d\vec E\), then integrate over the source coordinates.

Three position vectors—keep them distinct

\(\mathbf r'\): the source location, which varies throughout the integration.

\(\mathbf r\): the observation point, held fixed while integrating.

\(\boldsymbol{\mathcal R}=\mathbf r-\mathbf r'\): the separation vector from source element to observation point, so \(\mathcal R=|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|\).

\[\boxed{\boldsymbol{\mathcal R}=\mathbf r-\mathbf r'\,,\qquad\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal R}}=\frac{\mathbf r-\mathbf r'}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}}\]

Visualize source location and observation point

Move the little charge element along a distribution. The observation point stays fixed, but the separation vector changes.

The charge element slides along the \(x\)-axis as \(x'\) changes.

  1. Apply Coulomb’s law to one \(dq\).
    \[d\vec E(\mathbf r)=\frac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{dq}{\mathcal R^2}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal R}}.\]
  2. For volume charge, write the source element.
    \[dq=\rho(\mathbf r')\,d\tau'.\]

    In Cartesian coordinates, \(d\tau'=dx'\,dy'\,dz'\).

  3. Integrate over the entire source distribution.
    \[\boxed{\vec E(\mathbf r)=\frac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\iiint_V\frac{\rho(\mathbf r')\left(\mathbf r-\mathbf r'\right)}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|^3}\,d\tau'}\]

    Equivalently, \(\vec E(\mathbf r)=k\int_V[\rho(\mathbf r')/\mathcal R^2]\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal R}}\,d\tau'\). The one-integral notation often used in lectures still denotes a volume integral when the differential is \(d\tau'\).

The most common integration error

Do not treat \(\mathcal R\) or \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal R}}\) as constants without a symmetry argument. Changing \(\mathbf r'\) generally changes both the magnitude and direction of the vector \(\mathbf r-\mathbf r'\). Only \(\mathbf r\), the selected field point, stays fixed.

08 / Choosing the source element

Charge density by dimension

One dimension · Line
\(dq=\lambda\,d\ell'\)

Linear density \(\lambda\), in \(\mathrm{C/m}\). For a straight segment along \(x\), \(d\ell'=dx'\).

Two dimensions · Surface
\(dq=\sigma\,dA'\)

Surface density \(\sigma\), in \(\mathrm{C/m^2}\).

Three dimensions · Volume
\(dq=\rho\,d\tau'\)

Volume density \(\rho\), in \(\mathrm{C/m^3}\).

Universal prescription
\(\vec E=k\int \frac{dq}{\mathcal R^2}\hat{\mathcal R}\)

Use the differential appropriate to your charge geometry.

Three corresponding field integrals

\[\begin{aligned}\text{Line:}\quad&\vec E(\mathbf r)=k\int_L\!\frac{\lambda(\mathbf r')\,(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|^3}\,d\ell',\\[5pt]\text{Surface:}\quad&\vec E(\mathbf r)=k\iint_S\!\frac{\sigma(\mathbf r')\,(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|^3}\,dA',\\[5pt]\text{Volume:}\quad&\vec E(\mathbf r)=k\iiint_V\!\frac{\rho(\mathbf r')\,(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|^3}\,d\tau'.\end{aligned}\]

Recipe for the next worked example

Sketch the charge geometry, place \(dq\) at \(\mathbf r'\), draw \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal R}\) toward the observation point, replace \(dq\) by the appropriate density times differential element, resolve field components, use symmetry, and only then integrate.

09 / Retrieval practice

Check your understanding

1. Two identical positive charges are placed symmetrically about the origin. Where does their field point on the perpendicular bisector for \(z>0\)?
Choose an answer to check the symmetry.

Equal horizontal field components cancel; vertical field components reinforce, leaving a \(+\hat{\mathbf j}\) field above the pair.

2. Which term determines the leading distant field for an electric dipole?
Think about the cancellation of the monopole term.

The total charge is zero, eliminating the \(1/z^2\) monopole contribution. The separated charges leave a \(1/z^3\) field at leading order.

3. When integrating over a volume distribution to find \(\vec E(\mathbf r)\), which quantity is held fixed?
Recall what the prime tells you.

The source point \(\mathbf r'\) is the integration variable. The observation point \(\mathbf r\) stays fixed, so the separation vector changes.

Final study summary

Coulomb: keep the sign of \(q\); \(\hat{\mathbf R}\) always points source-to-field. Superposition: sum vectors. Equal charges: vertical components add, \(E\sim z^{-2}\) far away. Dipole: horizontal components add, \(E\sim z^{-3}\) far away. Continuous charge: replace \(dq\) by \(\lambda d\ell'\), \(\sigma dA'\), or \(\rho d\tau'\), then integrate over sources.
