01 / Foundations

Continuous charge distributions

From Coulomb's law to an integral

For an infinitesimal charge element \(dq\), the electric field at an observation point is

\[d\vec E=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{dq}{r^2}\,\hat{\mathbf r}\]

Here \(r\) is the separation from source to observation point, and \(\hat{\mathbf r}\) points from the source element toward that point. The sign of \(dq\) determines the field direction.

Charge density depends on geometry

Line charge

\[dq=\lambda\,d\ell\]

\([\lambda]=\mathrm{C/m}\)

Surface charge

\[dq=\sigma\,dA\]

\([\sigma]=\mathrm{C/m^2}\)

Volume charge

\[dq=\rho\,d\tau\]

\([\rho]=\mathrm{C/m^3}\)

General vector form

\[\vec E(\mathbf r)=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{\mathbf r-\mathbf r'}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|^3}\,dq\]

In the vector formula, \(\mathbf r'\) is the source position, and \(\mathbf r\) is the observation position. The density can be uniform or position-dependent: \(\lambda(x)\), \(\sigma(\mathbf r')\), or \(\rho(\mathbf r')\).

Watch the notation

Do not confuse the field-point coordinate \(\mathbf r\) with the separation \(|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|\). For a straight line along \(x\), \(d\ell=dx\), but this is not true for every curved line.

02 / Worked example

Uniformly charged finite line

A segment from \(x=-L\) to \(x=L\) carries uniform charge density \(\lambda\). Find the electric field at \(P=(0,z)\), with \(z>0\).

Explore the geometry

Change the half-length and observation height; horizontal components cancel in pairs.

Use symmetry before integrating

  1. Choose source element. At position \(x\), \(dq=\lambda\,dx\) and \(r=\sqrt{x^2+z^2}\).
  2. Resolve its field. The displacement from source to \(P\) is \(-x\hat{\mathbf x}+z\hat{\mathbf z}\). Hence \(d\vec E=\dfrac{\lambda\,dx}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{-x\hat{\mathbf x}+z\hat{\mathbf z}}{(x^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\).
  3. Pair \(+x\) and \(-x\). Their \(x\)-components cancel; \(z\)-components add. Integrate only \(0\le x\le L\), multiplying by 2.
\[d\vec E_{\text{pair}}=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2\lambda z\,dx}{(z^2+x^2)^{3/2}}\,\hat{\mathbf z}\]
\[\vec E=\frac{2\lambda z}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\hat{\mathbf z}\int_0^L\frac{dx}{(z^2+x^2)^{3/2}}\]

Evaluate the integral, step by step

  1. Let \(x=z\tan\theta\), so \(dx=z\sec^2\theta\,d\theta\).
  2. Since \(z>0\), \((z^2+x^2)^{3/2}=z^3\sec^3\theta\). Thus
    \[\int\frac{dx}{(z^2+x^2)^{3/2}}=\frac{1}{z^2}\int\cos\theta\,d\theta.\]
  3. The limits become \(\theta=0\) at \(x=0\), and \(\theta=\arctan(L/z)\) at \(x=L\).
  4. Therefore \[\frac{1}{z^2}\left[\sin\theta\right]_0^{\arctan(L/z)}=\frac{L}{z^2\sqrt{z^2+L^2}}.\]
\[\boxed{\vec E(0,z)=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2\lambda L}{z\sqrt{z^2+L^2}}\,\hat{\mathbf z}}\]

Because \(Q=2\lambda L\), another form is

\[\boxed{\vec E(0,z)=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{z\sqrt{z^2+L^2}}\,\hat{\mathbf z}}\]

For \(\lambda<0\), the field points downward. The vector formula preserves the sign automatically.

03 / Physical interpretation

Checks and limiting cases

Far from the line: \(z\gg L\)

Write the exact field in terms of \(Q\) and factor out \(z\):

\[\vec E=\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0z^2}\left(1+\frac{L^2}{z^2}\right)^{-1/2}\hat{\mathbf z}.\]

Using \((1+u)^n\approx1+nu\) for \(|u|\ll1\),

\[\vec E\approx\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0z^2}\left(1-\frac{L^2}{2z^2}\right)\hat{\mathbf z}.\]

The leading term is the point-charge field. The negative correction arises because an extended distribution is not concentrated at its midpoint.

Infinite-line limit: \(L\to\infty\)

\[\boxed{\vec E_{\infty}=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0z}\,\hat{\mathbf z}}\]

Hold \(\lambda\) and \(z\) fixed while allowing \(L\to\infty\). Even though the total charge diverges, the perpendicular field converges and varies as \(1/z\).

How the finite line approaches an infinite line

Plot the dimensionless ratio \(E_{\rm finite}/E_{\infty}=L/\sqrt{z^2+L^2}\).

As \(L/z\) increases, the ratio approaches 1 from below. For any finite length, the finite-line field is smaller than the infinite-line value at the same \(\lambda\) and \(z\).

Sanity checks

Direction: along the perpendicular bisector. Units: \([\lambda/\varepsilon_0 z]=\mathrm{N/C}\). Far field: \(1/z^2\) if total \(Q\) is fixed. Infinite line: \(1/z\) if density \(\lambda\) is fixed.

04 / Visual language

Electric-field lines

A field line is tangent to the net electric-field vector at every point. Its orientation shows direction, while line density qualitatively indicates field strength.

Field lines for point charges

Switch between a positive charge, a negative charge, and two equal positive charges.

The electric field points radially outward from a positive charge.

Rules for interpreting field lines

  • Lines leave positive charges and terminate on negative charges, or extend to/from infinity.
  • More lines represent greater charge magnitude under a consistent drawing convention.
  • Field lines never cross: the net field cannot have two directions at one point.
  • Closer line spacing represents a stronger field in qualitative diagrams.
  • For two equal positive charges, \(\vec E=0\) exactly at the midpoint. No field line runs through this null point.
05 / Geometric intuition

Electric flux

Flux measures the oriented amount of electric field crossing a surface:

\[\boxed{\Phi_E=\int_S\vec E\cdot d\vec A}\qquad\boxed{\Phi_E=\oint_S\vec E\cdot d\vec A\ \text{(closed surface)}}\]

Each area element is \(d\vec A=\hat{\mathbf n}\,dA\), so its contribution is

\[d\Phi_E=E\,dA\cos\theta\]

Rotate a surface in a uniform field

The angle is between \(\vec E\) and the chosen area normal, not the surface itself.

\(\Phi_E/EA=\cos\theta\) for a flat surface in a uniform field.

Why an exterior charge produces zero net closed-surface flux

Field lines crossing into a closed surface must later cross out. The incoming contribution is negative and the outgoing contribution is positive. They cancel in the total flux—even though the field at the surface may be nonzero.

06 / Maxwell's equations

Gauss’s law

\[\boxed{\oint_S\vec E\cdot d\vec A=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}}\]

For any closed surface, the net outward electric flux is proportional to the total charge enclosed. The geometry or shape of the surface does not affect this total; only \(Q_{\mathrm{enc}}\) does.

When Gauss’s law helps find \(\vec E\)

The law is always true, but it gives a simple expression for \(E\) only when symmetry makes the flux integral easy. Choose a Gaussian surface adapted to the symmetry.

  • Spherical symmetry: a concentric sphere.
  • Cylindrical symmetry: a coaxial cylinder.
  • Planar symmetry: a pillbox spanning the plane.

Example: Infinite line using a Gaussian cylinder

  1. Take a cylinder of radius \(s\) and length \(\ell\), centered on the line charge.
  2. By cylindrical symmetry, \(\vec E\) is radial with constant magnitude on the curved wall. The flux through the end caps is zero.
  3. The curved area is \(2\pi s\ell\), and the enclosed charge is \(\lambda\ell\).
  4. Apply Gauss’s law: \[E(2\pi s\ell)=\frac{\lambda\ell}{\varepsilon_0}.\]
\[\boxed{\vec E(s)=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 s}\,\hat{\mathbf s}}\]

This agrees with the \(L\to\infty\) limit of the Coulomb integral above.

Differential form

With the divergence theorem, Gauss’s law becomes

\[\boxed{\nabla\cdot\vec E=\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}}\]

This is one of Maxwell’s equations and remains valid for time-varying electromagnetic fields. Coulomb’s static-field formula is the electrostatic case, not the complete law for arbitrary time-dependent fields.

Multiple mathematical formulations, one physical world

Coulomb integration and Gauss’s law describe compatible physics, but each emphasizes different structure. Similarly, Newtonian, Lagrangian, and Hamiltonian mechanics—and Schrödinger, matrix, and path-integral quantum mechanics—offer different mathematical viewpoints on the same underlying phenomena.

Final summary
